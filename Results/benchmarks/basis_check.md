# Basis check — DFT training labels vs experimental benchmark labels

GENERATED by `scripts/basis_check.py` — do not hand-edit.

The models are trained on **Materials Project DFT-relaxed** lattice parameters.
The benchmark `a_true` columns are **experimental**. These are different quantities,
so part of every benchmark error is basis mismatch, not model error.

> MP's `theoretical: False` means the structure has been **observed**, NOT that the
> lattice parameters were **measured**. All MP lattice parameters are computed.

### Why no correction factor is shipped

1. **n = 3.** Three anchors cannot define a correction over a 64,128-row training domain.
2. **The sign flips** (UN −0.006621, UC −0.022736, CeO2 **+0.057365**). A single global
   offset is refuted by our own three points. Their mean is meaningless and is not computed.
3. **The delta is not attributable.** It inseparably bundles exchange-correlation functional
   error, 0 K (DFT) vs ~room-temperature (experiment) thermal expansion, zero-point motion,
   and error in the experimental value itself. Three numbers cannot separate four causes.

A defensible correction would need an independent, citable experimental reference set
covering a meaningful share of the training domain, at a stated temperature, with held-out
validation. That does not exist in this repo, so the correction is **not** asserted.

## (B) Basis-invariant model metrics

A constant offset **cannot change the slope or the correlation** of predicted-vs-true.
So these measure what each model learned about *composition* — and they stay valid
no matter what the DFT/experiment delta turns out to be.

`bias` is the mean signed error (the offset). `scatter` is the std of the error after
removing that bias — i.e. how well the model tracks the trend once the offset is gone.

### Reading these

- **MAE vs exp** is NOT pure model error — it contains the basis mismatch. Do not quote it
  as "the model is wrong by X Å" without saying what it is being compared against.
- **slope / Pearson r** are the defensible comparison. A model with a large bias but
  slope ≈ 1 and r ≈ 1 has *learned the composition dependence* and is merely offset.
  A model with small bias but r ≈ 0 has learned **nothing** and is landing near the right
  answer by coincidence.

**Independence caveat.** `Data/README.md` records UNUC.csv as "based on an experimental
fit" — `a_true` there is a smooth fitted curve sampled at many compositions, not that many
independent measurements. Correlation against it measures fidelity to that fit.
CeO2Nd2O3Vals.csv has n=7, so its slope carries real uncertainty. Weigh both with their n.
